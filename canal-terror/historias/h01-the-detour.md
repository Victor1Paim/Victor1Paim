I'd been driving for almost eleven hours when the detour signs pushed me off the interstate. The road narrowed to two lanes, then the radio lost its station, and for a long time the only light out there was my own headlights. Around two in the morning I saw a gas station sign glowing over the fields. The pumps were on. The store was dark. And there was one other car parked by the air machine, with its engine running and nobody inside.

This was in October, about six years ago. I was twenty-four and moving back home to Missouri after a job in Colorado fell apart. Everything I owned was in the back seat of a Corolla with a cracked windshield. I'd planned to stop for the night in a motel somewhere in western Kansas, but I kept telling myself one more hour, and by the time the detour came up I was too tired to be smart about it.

The last real town I passed was a place with one stoplight and a grain elevator lit up like a ship. After that, the map on my phone turned into a gray blank with a single blue line through it. When the detour started, there was a pickup truck ahead of me for a while, the only other headlights for miles. At a crossroads with no signs at all, it turned onto a dirt road and its lights went out almost immediately, like it had pulled over and shut off the engine. I remember thinking that was a strange place to stop. I kept going straight, because the orange arrows told me to.

I pulled up to the pump farthest from the other car. It was an older station wagon, dark green, with wood panels on the sides and a bumper sticker that said "Proud Parent of an Honor Student" with the name of the school scratched off. The exhaust was rising in the cold. The headlights were off, but the dome light was on, and I could see a jacket on the passenger seat and a fast food bag on the dashboard. I figured whoever it belonged to was in the bathroom around the side of the building.

Then I noticed the back seat. There was a child's booster seat behind the driver, and a stuffed rabbit sitting in it, turned around so it faced the rear window. A pair of small sneakers sat on the floor mat underneath, neatly side by side, like someone had taken them off and planned to put them right back on. Whoever had been driving that car hadn't been alone.

I filled the tank and waited. I don't know why I waited. I think I wanted to see someone walk back to that car, just so I could stop thinking about it. Nobody came. The pump clicked off, the screen asked if I wanted a receipt, and the whole time that engine kept idling, steady, like it had been running for hours.

I didn't go check the bathroom. I got back in my car and left.

As I pulled away, I looked in the rearview mirror one last time. The dome light in the wagon was still on. The stuffed rabbit in the back seat was facing forward now.

About twenty minutes later the detour signs ended at a truck stop. It was the first real building I'd seen in a long time. A diner on one side, a couple of semis parked out back with their running lights on, and a sign that said Open 24 Hours with two of the letters burned out. I parked near the entrance because I needed coffee and I needed to sit somewhere with people for a while.

Inside it was warm and smelled like burnt coffee and bacon grease. There was a trucker at the counter reading his phone, an older man asleep in a booth by the window, and one waitress. Her name tag said Dee. She looked about fifty and she called me honey before I'd even sat down. I took a booth facing the door, ordered coffee and a slice of pie, and started looking at the map on my phone, trying to figure out how far I was from anything.

That's when the couple came in.

They were young, maybe my age. The guy wore a gray hoodie and the girl had on a denim jacket that was too thin for the weather. It had started to rain outside, and the shoulders of their jackets were dark with it. They were arguing in that low, tight way people argue in public. As they walked past my booth, the girl said, "Every single time, Ray. Every single time." He didn't answer. They sat two booths down from me, across the aisle, and Dee went over with menus and two waters like it was nothing.

I didn't think much about it. I ate my pie. I heard bits of their conversation, something about a wrong turn and whose idea it had been to leave so late. The girl had a laugh that wasn't really a laugh. At one point Ray said, "We'll be fine. We're almost there." She didn't say anything back.

Dee came by to refill my coffee and asked where I was headed. When I told her Missouri, she nodded like she'd heard it a thousand times. Then she glanced at the window and lowered her voice a little. She said, "When you leave, honey, follow the signs back to the interstate. Don't take the old county road, even if your phone tells you it's shorter." I asked her why. She just smiled, said the road wasn't kept up anymore, and went back behind the counter.

After a while I got up to use the restroom. It was at the end of a short hallway past the kitchen. I remember the hallway because one of the fluorescent lights was flickering, and there was a corkboard on the wall with business cards and a flyer for a missing dog. I was in there maybe three or four minutes. I washed my hands, splashed water on my face, and looked at myself in the mirror, thinking I really needed to find a bed.

When I came out and walked back toward my booth, the bell over the front door rang.

It was them. The same couple. Gray hoodie, thin denim jacket, the shoulders of both jackets dark with rain. They walked in the same way, him a step ahead, her a little behind with her arms crossed. And as they passed me, the girl said, "Every single time, Ray. Every single time."

I stopped in the aisle. I looked over at the booth two down from mine, where they'd been sitting. It was empty. No menus, no glasses, no wet rings on the table. It had been wiped down like nobody had used it all night.

They sat in that same booth. Dee came over with two menus and two waters, and she smiled at them, and she said, "Evening, you two. Rough night out there?" Exactly how you'd greet someone who just walked in.

I stood there long enough that I felt stupid, so I made myself walk past their booth on the way back to mine. Up close, I could see the girl's jacket. The rain had soaked into the denim in exactly the same shape as before, a dark patch across both shoulders and a long streak down one arm. Ray had his wrist on the table, and his watch caught the light. It was one of those old digital watches. It read 2:14, and while I watched, it didn't change.

I sat down because I didn't know what else to do. My coffee was still there. My plate was still there. I watched them. I kept waiting for one of them to look at me, or laugh, or for this to turn into some kind of joke. Ray was rubbing his eyes. The girl was looking out the window at the parking lot. They were having the same conversation. Wrong turn. Leaving too late. And then Ray said, "We'll be fine. We're almost there."

I looked at the trucker at the counter. He hadn't looked up from his phone the whole time. The old man in the window booth was still asleep. Nobody else in that room seemed to think anything was wrong.

I don't know where the nerve came from, but I walked over to their booth. I stood at the end of the table and asked, as normally as I could, if they'd just gotten there. The girl didn't look at me. She kept staring at the parking lot. Ray looked up, and for a second I thought he was going to say something real. His eyes were red, like he hadn't slept in a very long time. Then he said, very calmly, "We'll be fine. We're almost there." He said it to me. Exactly the way he'd said it to her.

I left a twenty on the table and walked out. I didn't wait for change. When I got outside, the rain had stopped, and the parking lot was dry. Completely dry. No puddles, nothing wet on my windshield. I stood there for a second looking at the ground, then I got in my car, locked the doors, and sat with the engine running for a long time. I didn't want to drive past that gas station again. I didn't want to go back inside either. So I just sat there until the sky started to turn gray, and then I followed the signs back to the interstate.

I didn't pass the gas station on the way back. I took the same road. I'm sure of that. It just wasn't there.

I had a dashcam back then, one of those cheap ones that records over itself every few hours. When I finally got home and checked it, most of that night had already been overwritten. The only file left starts as I pull into the truck stop parking lot at 2:14 in the morning. I park, the camera keeps recording, and about a minute later the dark green station wagon pulls into the spot right in front of me. Engine running. Dome light on. Nobody inside.

The date on that clip is October 14th. The year is the one before I ever drove that road.

I still drive that interstate a couple of times a year. If the detour signs are up, I don't take them. I find the last town before the exit, I park somewhere well lit, and I sleep in my car until morning.
