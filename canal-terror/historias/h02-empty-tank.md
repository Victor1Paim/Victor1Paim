The woman who tried to get into my car that night was very polite about it. I want to start there, because it's the part people never believe.

I ran out of gas on a state highway in Nevada a little after ten at night, about forty miles from the nearest town. That part was my fault. I was twenty-two, driving from Reno to see my sister in Arizona, and I'd skipped the last station because the prices were ridiculous and I was sure there'd be another one soon. There wasn't.

The car started sputtering on a long, straight stretch with nothing on either side but sagebrush and dark hills. I coasted onto the shoulder, turned on my hazards, and called roadside assistance with my one bar of signal. The woman on the phone said a tow truck would be there in about ninety minutes. She asked if I felt safe. I said yes, because what else are you supposed to say.

When the engine stopped ticking, there was nothing. No wind, no insects, no traffic. Around eleven I noticed a light out in the desert, far off to the right, low to the ground, like a lantern. It didn't move for a long time. Then it went out, and a few seconds later it came back on, closer. After that I stopped looking at it, because every time I looked, it was a little nearer.

About thirty minutes in, headlights came up behind me. My stomach dropped, and then I saw the light bar on the roof. It was a sheriff's deputy, an older guy with a gray mustache and a tired face. He brought me two bottles of water from his car. Then he leaned down to my window, and his voice changed. He said, "Keep your doors locked until the tow gets here. If anybody walks up to this car, you don't roll the window down. You call 911 and you tell them Deputy Harlan was out here." He looked past me, into the back of my car, for a second longer than he needed to. "And whatever you do, don't let anybody in the back."

I asked him if something had happened. He looked down the road for a second. "People walk this road at night sometimes," he said. "I don't stop for them anymore." Then he got back in his cruiser and drove off, and I watched his taillights until they were one red dot, and then nothing.

That's when I saw the light in the desert again. It was much closer now, maybe a hundred yards off the road, right about level with my car. It stayed on for a few seconds, long enough for me to see that it was swinging a little, the way a lantern swings when someone is holding it. Then it went out, and it didn't come back on.

I called my sister. The line was full of static, like she was standing in wind. I told her I'd run out of gas and a deputy had stopped, and that I was fine. Then she asked who was with me. I said nobody. She was quiet for a second, and then she said, "There's someone talking. It sounds like they're right behind you." The call dropped. I turned around and looked at my back seat. A blanket, a duffel bag, nothing else. I couldn't get her back.

I locked the doors and checked them twice. I sat with my phone in my lap and watched the side mirrors. The road behind me was a straight black line under the stars, and after about a quarter mile it disappeared into the dark. Then I heard gravel crunch somewhere behind the car. Just once, the way it sounds when someone shifts their weight. I turned on my headlights and stared into the mirrors until my eyes watered. There was nothing there. I told myself it was a coyote. I noticed I'd stopped breathing while I listened, and I had to make myself start again. When I looked up at the rearview mirror again, the blanket that had been folded on my back seat was on the floor. I told myself it had slid off when I braked. I'd been parked for over an hour.

The tow truck showed up about an hour after the deputy left. I almost cried when I saw the amber lights. The driver, a big guy in a reflective vest named Manny, backed up in front of my car and told me to stay inside while he hooked it up. I watched him in my headlights, crouched at my front bumper, working with the chains. Then I looked in my rearview mirror.

There was a woman standing behind my car.

She was on the shoulder, maybe ten feet back, at the edge of the red glow from my taillights. She was older, maybe sixties, thin, with long gray hair and a hiking backpack. She wasn't wearing a coat, and it was close to freezing. I hadn't seen headlights. I hadn't heard footsteps. She was just there, with her hands at her sides, completely still, the way people stand in an elevator waiting for the doors to open.

I didn't move. I watched her walk up along the passenger side, slowly, and stop at the back door. Then she reached out and pulled the handle.

It was locked. She pulled it again, harder, and the whole car rocked. Then she bent down and looked through the back window. She didn't look at me at all. She was looking at the back seat, with this patient, almost hopeful expression, like she was waiting for someone back there to sit up and let her in.

I hit the horn. Manny stood up in front of the car. The woman straightened, came around to my window, and knocked on the glass with one knuckle. Very politely. She leaned down and said, "My car's just up the road. I've got a flat. I just need a ride to it."

Her voice was completely normal, a little hoarse, like somebody's grandmother. I shook my head. She smiled and said, "It's right up there," and pointed down the highway, the way I'd been driving.

Manny was coming around the front of the car, saying, "Hey. Hey, ma'am." I looked at him for maybe a second. When I looked back at the window, she was gone.

I turned all the way around in my seat. For one second, in the dark of the back seat, I thought someone was sitting up straight behind the passenger seat, small, with their head turned toward the window. Then Manny's flashlight swept across the glass, and it was only my duffel bag. The shoulder was empty. The road was empty. Manny walked around my car with his flashlight and then fifty yards back along the shoulder. He got down on one knee and shined the light under my car, the way you'd check under a bed. There were no footprints, no sound, nothing. The desert was flat for miles, and there was nowhere for a person to go.

When he came back, he shined his light on the back door, where she'd pulled the handle. Then he moved the light up to the rear window and kept it there. There were handprints on the glass. Small ones, smeared, and a lot of them. He leaned closer, then looked at me, and I saw his face change. The prints were on the inside.

He opened my door and told me to ride up in the truck with him, and he didn't make it sound like a question. I didn't take my bag. I didn't look at the back seat again.

Manny drove fast, faster than I think a tow truck is supposed to, with both hands on the wheel, and he kept checking the mirror on his side. We drove for about twenty minutes before either of us said anything. We passed the place where she'd pointed, and the next five miles after that. There was no car on the side of that road.

At some point I looked in the big side mirror on my door. My car was right behind us on the tow, bouncing a little on the chains. The dome light inside it was on. I hadn't touched it. I hadn't been in that car since Manny opened my door. I watched it for maybe a mile, and then the light went off, the way it does when someone closes a door. Manny saw me looking. He reached over and tilted my mirror down so I couldn't see it.

Finally Manny said, "She tell you it was a flat?" I said yes. He nodded and kept his eyes on the road. "You're the third one this year she's told that to," he said. "The other two opened the door."

I asked him what happened to them. He didn't answer me. He just turned the radio on.
