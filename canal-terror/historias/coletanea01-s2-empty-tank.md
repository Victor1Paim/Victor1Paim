# Coletânea 01 · Story 2 — "Empty Tank" (rascunho 1)

- Original; semente: relato da mulher que fica sem gasolina no deserto e vê uma mulher aparecer e tentar abrir a porta de trás (achado pelo Grok no X). Nomes, lugar, falas e final são nossos.
- Meta: ~1.300 palavras.

---

I ran out of gas on a state highway in Nevada a little after ten at night, about forty miles from the nearest town. That part was my fault. I was twenty-two, driving from Reno to see my sister in Arizona, and I'd skipped the last station because the prices were ridiculous and I was sure there'd be another one soon. There wasn't.

The car started sputtering on a long, straight stretch with nothing on either side but sagebrush and dark hills. I coasted onto the shoulder and sat there with my hazards on, listening to the engine tick as it cooled. I had one bar of signal. I called roadside assistance, gave them the mile marker from my map app, and the woman on the phone told me a tow truck would be there in about ninety minutes. She asked if I felt safe. I said yes, because what else are you supposed to say.

The first thing I noticed was how quiet it was. When the engine stopped ticking there was nothing. No wind, no insects, no traffic. I could hear my own breathing. Every few minutes I turned the key just far enough to check the time on the dashboard, then turned it off again to save the battery. I had half a bottle of water and a bag of sunflower seeds, and I remember rationing the water like I was in a survival movie, which is funny now.

About thirty minutes in, headlights came up behind me. My stomach dropped, but then I saw the light bar on the roof. It was a sheriff's deputy. He pulled in behind me and walked up with a flashlight, an older guy with a gray mustache and a tired, friendly face. I explained what happened. He shook his head and laughed a little, not in a mean way, and said I wasn't the first and wouldn't be the last.

He went back to his car and came back with two bottles of water. Then he leaned down to my window and his voice changed a little. He said, "Listen. Keep your doors locked until the tow gets here. If anybody walks up to the car, you don't roll the window down. You call 911 and you tell them Deputy Harlan was out here."

I asked him if something had happened out here. He looked down the road for a second, then back at me. He said, "People walk this road at night sometimes. I don't stop for them anymore." Then he told me to have a good night, got back in his cruiser, and pulled away. I watched his taillights for a long time, until they turned into one red dot and then nothing.

After that, the quiet felt different.

I locked the doors and checked them twice. I sat with my phone in my lap and kept looking in the side mirrors. The road behind me was a straight black line under the stars. I could see maybe a quarter mile back, and then it disappeared into the dark. I told myself the deputy was just being careful, that he probably said that to everyone. I tried playing a podcast but I turned it off after a minute because I didn't like not being able to hear.

The tow truck showed up about an hour after the deputy left. I almost cried when I saw the amber lights. The driver backed up in front of my car, got out, and gave me a wave. He was a big guy in a reflective vest, probably in his fifties. His name was Manny. He told me to stay in the car while he hooked it up, it would only take a few minutes. I nodded and stayed put.

I was watching him in my headlights, crouched down at the front of my car, working with the chains. And then I looked in my rearview mirror.

There was a woman standing behind my car.

She wasn't on the road. She was on the shoulder, maybe ten feet back, at the edge of the red glow from my taillights. She was older, maybe sixties, thin, with long gray hair and a hiking backpack. She wasn't wearing a coat, and it was cold enough that I had the heat on until the battery scared me. I hadn't seen any headlights. I hadn't heard footsteps on the gravel. She was just there.

I didn't move. I watched her in the mirror. She walked up along the passenger side of the car, slowly, and stopped at the back door. Then she reached out and pulled the handle.

It was locked. She pulled it again, harder, and the whole car rocked a little. Then she bent down and looked through the back window. She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the back seat, like she expected someone to be sitting there.

I hit the horn. I don't know why, it was the only thing I could think of. Manny stood up in front of the car. The woman straightened up and walked to my window, the passenger side, and knocked on the glass with one knuckle. Very politely. She leaned down and said, "My car's just up the road. I've got a flat. I just need a ride to it."

Her voice was normal. That's what I keep coming back to. It was a completely normal voice, a little hoarse, like somebody's grandmother. I shook my head. She smiled at me and said, "It's right up there," and pointed down the highway, in the direction I'd been driving.

Manny was walking around the front of my car now, saying, "Hey. Hey, ma'am." I looked at him for maybe a second. When I looked back at the window, she wasn't there.

I turned all the way around in my seat. The shoulder was empty. The road was empty. Manny walked all the way around my car with his flashlight. He walked back along the shoulder maybe fifty yards. There was nothing out there. No footprints that we could see, no sound, nothing. The desert was flat for miles. There wasn't anywhere for a person to go.

He came back to my window and asked if I was okay. I told him what she'd said, that her car was just up the road with a flat. He didn't say anything for a second. Then he asked me to ride up in the truck with him, and he didn't really make it sound like a question.

We drove for about twenty minutes before either of us talked. We passed the place where she'd pointed. We passed the next five miles after that. There was no car on the side of that road. There was nothing.

Finally Manny said, "She tell you it was a flat?" I said yes. He nodded and kept his eyes on the road. He said, "You're the third one this year she's told that to. The other two opened the door."

I asked him what happened to them. He didn't answer me. He just turned the radio on.
