# Coletânea 01 · Story 3 — "The Receipt" (rascunho 1)

- Original; semente: relatos curtos de mulheres seguidas na estrada que param num truck stop e são ajudadas por um caminhoneiro (achados pelo Grok no X). Nomes, lugar, falas e final são nossos.
- Meta: ~1.300 palavras.

---

The summer after my freshman year of college, I drove home alone from Denver to Indianapolis. It's about sixteen hours, and I'd convinced my parents I could do it in one day if I left early enough. My dad made me promise to text him every time I stopped for gas. I thought he was being dramatic. I was nineteen and I'd never had anything bad happen to me, which I think is the only reason anybody is ever that confident.

I left at five in the morning. Everything went fine through eastern Colorado and most of Kansas. I listened to an entire audiobook. I stopped for gas outside of Salina around six in the evening, texted my dad a picture of the pump like I'd promised, bought a coffee and a bag of beef jerky, and got back on I-70. By the time it got dark I was somewhere in eastern Kansas, and the traffic had thinned out to almost nothing. Just me and the occasional semi.

That's when I noticed the headlights.

They were behind me, not close, maybe a few hundred feet back. At first I didn't think about it. But I was driving a little under the limit because I was tired, and normally people would pass me. These didn't. When I slowed down, they slowed down. When I sped up to see what would happen, they sped up, and kept the exact same distance, like they were attached to my car on a string.

I changed lanes. A few seconds later, so did they. I changed back. So did they. I told myself it was a coincidence, that somebody else was just tired and following my taillights, which people do. But it kept going. Twenty minutes. Forty minutes. An hour. Every time I checked the mirror, they were in the same spot. Two round headlights, a little dimmer on the left side.

I called my dad. I tried to sound calm, but I don't think I did. He told me not to get off at an empty exit, not to go home, and to find the biggest, brightest place I could and pull in right up front. I opened my map and there was a truck stop about twelve miles ahead. He stayed on the phone with me the whole way. Every few minutes he asked, "Are they still there?" And every time I said yes.

The truck stop was huge. Rows of semis in the back, a restaurant, a convenience store, bright white lights over everything. I pulled into a spot right in front of the store, close enough that I could see the cashier through the window. I watched my mirror.

The car pulled in a few seconds later. It drove slowly past the front of the store, past me, and all the way to the far end of the lot, where the lights didn't reach very well. It parked facing my direction. Then its headlights turned off.

Nobody got out.

I sat there for ten minutes with my dad on the phone. The car didn't move. I couldn't see what kind of car it was, just a dark shape, maybe a sedan. I couldn't see anyone inside it. My dad said to go inside and tell someone, so I did. I almost ran into the store.

The cashier was a teenage kid who didn't really know what to do with me. But there was a trucker at the coffee station who'd heard me, an older man in a Carhartt jacket with a white beard and reading glasses pushed up on his head. He asked me which car. I pointed it out through the window. He looked at it for a long time. Then he said, "Okay. Where are you headed?"

His name was Walt. He told me he was going east anyway, and he'd follow right behind me to the next big town, about forty miles. He said if that car tried anything, it would have to deal with eighty thousand pounds of truck first. He gave me his CB channel, but I didn't have a CB, so we just traded phone numbers. My dad talked to him on my phone for a minute. I don't know what they said, but after that my dad sounded a lot calmer.

We pulled out together. My car, then Walt's truck, then nothing. I watched the far end of the lot as we passed, and the dark car was still sitting there with its lights off.

About five miles down the highway, the headlights came back.

They came up fast this time, much faster than before. I saw them grow in my mirror, past Walt's truck, and I felt my whole body go cold. Then Walt moved. He swung his truck into the left lane, right next to the car, and stayed there, matching its speed. Every time it tried to get around him, he drifted over and blocked it. He called me and said, very calmly, "Just keep driving, sweetheart. Don't speed. I've got him."

It went on for maybe two or three minutes. Then the car braked hard, dropped back, and took the next exit. Walt stayed behind me for the next forty miles. He followed me into the parking lot of a police station in the next town, waited while I went in, and then he flashed his lights twice and pulled back onto the highway. I never saw him again. I wish I'd gotten his last name.

I told the officer at the desk everything. I didn't have a plate number, and I didn't really expect anything to come of it. He took my information, gave me a coffee, and let me sit in the lobby until I felt okay to drive the last few hours home. My dad met me halfway.

Two days later, a detective from Kansas called me.

He said a patrol car had found a dark gray sedan abandoned at that same truck stop the next morning, parked at the far end of the lot. The car had been reported stolen four days earlier from a woman in Colorado. The woman had also been reported missing. He asked me a lot of questions about what I saw, and I didn't have many answers.

Then he asked me if I'd bought gas in Salina that evening. I said yes. I'd texted my dad a picture of the pump, so I even knew the exact time.

He said they'd found a receipt on the passenger seat of that car. It was from the same gas station in Salina. Pump four, the pump next to mine.

It was printed one minute after my receipt.
