# História 03 — "The Note Under the Door"

- Tema: Home Alone. Original: escrita a partir de elementos comuns do gênero (bilhete do vizinho, passos de madrugada,
  câmera, intruso escondido na casa). Nenhum relato específico copiado.
- Formato: vídeo próprio (~10 min). Voz Gemini Enceladus. Cartela: "The Note Under the Door / By: Anonymous".

---

The first note was taped to my door on a Tuesday morning in February. It was written in blue ink on the back of a grocery receipt, in careful, slanted handwriting. It said, "Please stop pacing at three in the morning. I can hear every step. Thank you. Ruth, downstairs."

I read it three times standing in the hallway with my keys in my hand. I wasn't annoyed. I was embarrassed. I was twenty-seven, I'd been living alone in that apartment for about four months, and I really liked Ruth. She was the older woman who lived in the downstairs unit of our duplex, a retired schoolteacher who always had her porch light on and her television a little too loud. The last thing I wanted was to be the noisy neighbor.

The problem was that I didn't pace. Not at three in the morning, not at any time. I worked early shifts doing admin at a hospital, and I was usually asleep by ten. I sleep like a rock. My sister used to say you could move my bed across the room with me in it and I wouldn't notice.

The apartment itself was the upstairs half of an old house in Columbus, Ohio. Hardwood floors, radiators that clanked every time the heat came on, one long hallway that ran from the living room past the bathroom to my bedroom at the back. There was a narrow coat closet in that hallway that I never used. The landlord told me when I moved in that there was a plumbing access panel at the back of it, and that if anything ever leaked, that was where the shutoff was. I'd opened the closet once, saw a painted square of plywood held in with screws, and closed it again.

That evening I knocked on Ruth's door to apologize anyway. She was sweet about it. She said she hadn't wanted to bother me, but it had been going on for a few weeks and she wasn't sleeping well. I asked her what exactly she was hearing. She thought about it for a second and said, "It's slow. Back and forth, from your living room down the hallway and back again. Like someone thinking about something." She said it usually started a little after three and went on for twenty minutes or so. Then it stopped, and she heard a door close.

I told her it couldn't have been me. I told her about the early shifts and how heavy a sleeper I was. She gave me this kind, patient look, the way you'd look at a student who swore they'd done their homework, and said maybe I was sleepwalking. She said her late husband used to do it, and he never believed her either.

I laughed it off, but it bothered me. When I got back upstairs I noticed little things I'd been ignoring. The thermostat was set to sixty-eight, and I always kept it at sixty-five because the heat was expensive. There was a gray hoodie I'd been looking for since Christmas, and I'd assumed I left it at my sister's. A container of leftover pasta I was sure I hadn't finished was gone from the fridge. Every one of those things had an easy explanation, and every one of them, on its own, was nothing. I told myself I was being dramatic.

But I did something about the sleepwalking idea. That night, before bed, I set my phone on the little table by the couch and started a voice recording, and I let it run all night.

In the morning I almost didn't listen to it. Seven hours of a quiet apartment didn't seem worth the time. But on my lunch break, I opened it and scrolled through the audio waveform, looking for anything that wasn't a flat line. Most of it was just the hum of the refrigerator and the radiator ticking. Then, at three eleven in the morning, the line started to move.

I put my earbuds in and pressed play. At first it was just a creak, the long, low creak an old floorboard makes when weight settles on it slowly. Then another one, a little closer. Then footsteps. Not the quick, careless steps of someone walking to the bathroom. Slow ones. Heel, then toe, like someone trying very hard not to make noise. They crossed the living room right past my phone, went down the hallway, and stopped.

I knew exactly where they stopped, because there's one board outside my bedroom door that pops when you step on it, and I heard it pop. Then nothing. For almost a full minute, there was no sound on the recording at all. And then, very faintly, I heard breathing. Slow and even, right outside my bedroom door. While I was asleep on the other side of it.

I sat in my car in the hospital parking garage and listened to that minute four times. Then the steps went back up the hallway, slowly, and I heard a soft click, like a door being pulled shut.

The only door in that hallway, besides mine and the bathroom, was the coat closet.

I called my sister, and she told me to call the police. I didn't, and I know how that sounds. I kept thinking about how it would go. A woman calls and says she heard footsteps on her own phone recording, in her own apartment, where she lives alone. They'd tell me I was sleepwalking, just like Ruth did. I wanted proof. Something no one could explain away.

So after work I drove to an electronics store and bought a cheap Wi-Fi camera, the kind people use to watch their dogs. I set it on top of the bookshelf in the living room, angled so it could see the couch, the kitchen doorway and the start of the hallway. I tested it with my phone. The picture was grainy, but in night mode you could see everything in shades of gray.

Then I packed a bag and went to sleep at my sister's place. I told myself it was an experiment. If the camera showed nothing, I'd go home, admit I was sleepwalking and buy Ruth a fruit basket. My sister made up the couch for me, and I lay there with my phone on my chest, opening the app every half hour to look at my empty living room.

I must have fallen asleep around one. The notification woke me up. "Motion detected, Living Room," at three fourteen a.m.

I opened the feed, and there was a man in my apartment.

He was standing in the middle of my living room, barefoot, in the gray hoodie I'd been missing since Christmas. He was thin, maybe in his forties, with his hair grown out and pushed behind his ears. He wasn't searching for anything. He wasn't stealing anything. He was just walking, very slowly, from the couch to the start of the hallway and back again, with his hands folded in front of him. Exactly the way Ruth described it. Like someone thinking about something.

At one point he stopped at the kitchen doorway and stood there with his head tilted, listening. Then he went into the kitchen, opened my refrigerator and stood in the light of it, eating something out of a container with his fingers. He put the container back. He wiped his hand on the hoodie. And then he walked down the hallway, opened the coat closet, stepped inside and pulled the door shut behind him.

I woke my sister up, and this time I called 911.

The officers who met me there an hour later were polite, and honestly, I think they expected to find nothing. They checked every room, every window and under my bed. Then I pointed at the coat closet, and one of them opened it with his flashlight out. The square of plywood at the back was still there. But the screws were gone. It was only leaning in place. When he pulled it away, there was a dark gap behind it, about two feet high, that ran between the floors of the house, above Ruth's ceiling and under mine.

They didn't find him. There was a vent at the far end of that crawl space that opened onto the side of the house, and its cover had been pried loose from the inside. But they found where he'd been living. A camping pad and a sleeping bag. Granola bar wrappers, and a lot of my food containers, cleaned and stacked. A phone charger plugged into an outlet that, it turned out, was on the back of my bedroom wall. And a spiral notebook.

One of the officers showed me a page of that notebook, and I wish he hadn't. It was my schedule. When I left for work, when I came home, which nights I did laundry, what time my bedroom light usually went off. On the last page, in very small, neat handwriting, it said, "She sleeps very deeply."

The detective who called me the next week said they had a name, a man who'd been evicted from a house two streets over the summer before. They believed he'd been living in that crawl space since before I signed my lease. Longer than I had lived there.

I broke my lease and moved in with my sister for a while. Ruth cried when I told her what happened. She kept saying she was sorry, that she should have known it wasn't me, that she should have said something sooner. I told her she was the only reason I ever found out at all.

They never found him. I check the news sometimes for his name. Nothing.

Ruth still sends me a card every Christmas. Last spring she called me, just to catch up. She told me the landlord had fixed up the apartment and a new girl had moved in upstairs, a nursing student. She said the girl was sweet and very quiet. She just wished she wouldn't pace so much at night.
